# Closing: States and Transitions

*A practical method for shaping the states we leave behind*

**Model type:** Application Model, with an orienting framework  
**Scope:** Resulting states in everyday activities, tasks, projects, and the handling of personal experiences  
**Status:** Exploratory Note  
**Version:** v0.1  
**Last updated:** 2026-11-25

## Abstract

This note describes a method of closing developed from my own experience. Its focus is the state that remains when an activity, attempt, project, or period of life ends, and how that state influences what follows.

Closing does not require success. Something may be completed, abandoned, or brought to an end without achieving its original aim. Something may also remain open for a useful reason.

The method directs attention toward what ends, what remains, and what is carried forward. It leaves the definition of a good state to the person applying it. The proposed benefit is a more deliberate handling of the conditions from which later action begins.

Its usefulness for other people and situations remains open to practical examination.

## Starting Point

Closing is an everyday occurrence. We finish conversations, put away laundry, correct errors, submit work, and stop working for the day. Most of these endings require no explicit reflection.

Each leaves a state behind. The next action starts from that state rather than from an empty beginning.

A dishwasher may be ready for dirty dishes or still contain clean ones. A project may be easy to resume or require reconstructing what happened. An unsuccessful attempt may leave a useful lesson, an unresolved question, or an expectation that influences the next attempt.

The method considers these resulting states and asks:

> What state remains when I stop, and what does that state make possible or necessary next?

## The Model

### 1. Resulting States Influence What Follows

A resulting state includes whatever matters for subsequent action: physical arrangements, completed work, remaining obligations, available information, expectations, and feelings.

These elements affect what can happen next. An unclear arrangement may require someone to reconstruct the situation before proceeding. A usable result may allow the next task to begin. A recorded insight may inform a later attempt.

The working relationship is:

> Action leaves a state. That state influences later action. Observing its effects can inform how future endings are handled.

The ending itself does not guarantee improvement. The method concerns the state produced and its expected effects.

### 2. Good States Depend on Goals and Circumstances

The method does not define a universally good state. People have different aims, needs, responsibilities, and resources.

A familiar state that has worked well before can provide orientation. So can an improvement over the previous state. Both require attention to the present circumstances.

Passing an examination may satisfy the relevant requirement. If a particular grade is needed for a scholarship, the same result may not satisfy that additional aim. The standard depends on the goal concerned.

More demanding goals need not raise the standard for everything. They may instead require concentrating effort on relevant tasks and handling other matters more simply or with assistance.

Personal priorities determine what is valued. Observations can still challenge factual expectations about how a chosen state will work.

### 3. Open and Closed Refer to a Particular Unit

For a specified matter, this model uses a binary distinction: open or closed.

A submitted examination attempt is closed. The examination requirement may remain open if another attempt is needed. The degree program may remain open in either case. Ending the program can close that larger undertaking without fulfilling its original aim.

Closure and achievement are therefore different properties.

A matter can also remain open while its current handling is clear. It may have a scheduled date, a responsible person, or a defined next step. Waiting can allow more information, a better opportunity, or further development.

Open matters can be overlooked or kept open to avoid an uncomfortable ending. Awareness alone does not establish whether waiting is useful. Reasons for keeping something open may need reconsideration as time, opportunities, and costs change.

### 4. Closable Units Can Be Designed or Discovered

Small tasks can often be completed directly. Larger tasks may be divided into parts that each produce a recognizable result and contribute to the whole. Integration work belongs among those parts when it is needed.

The full extent of a task may become apparent only during work. When clearing a room, for example, sorting one cupboard can produce a sensible stopping point even though much remains to be done.

A stable intermediate state can be left for a time without creating an immediate need for further work. It can support a pause while the larger task remains open.

For extended work, pace and scope should account for the effort still required. Opening more work than can be left in a usable state may create additional burdens at the stopping point.

### 5. Experience Can Be Carried Across Endings

An ending may leave knowledge or a changed expectation alongside its practical result.

Someone makes an error, recognizes it, and identifies an adjustment. That attempt can be closed before the adjustment has been tested. A later attempt can show whether the lesson was useful.

The earlier error remains an error. Its significance can nevertheless change as it becomes part of a demonstrated improvement.

Personal endings may also involve feelings and a workable interpretation of what happened. A decision to stop does not necessarily complete that process. An outward ending and a person's inner relationship to it may develop at different times.

Nothing here requires extracting a new lesson from every routine action or making feelings disappear before moving on.

## Practical Use

The explanation can be compressed into four working questions:

1. **Recognize the state:** What will remain if I stop now?
2. **Assess the state:** Does it fit my goals and the reasonably foreseeable requirements that follow?
3. **Shape the state:** What further action is worthwhile, or why should the matter remain open?
4. **Learn from the effects:** What does later experience reveal about that assessment?

Use these questions proportionally. Familiar routines may need only a glance. A consequential ending may justify more examination. The handling of the ending should not become an indefinitely expanding task of its own.

For physical arrangements and shared processes, one practical heuristic is:

> If an uninvolved person can quickly recognize the state and understand how to proceed, that is often an indication of a usable state.

Recognizability is a clue rather than a complete definition of quality. It does not determine whether a personal or emotional ending is satisfactory.

## Illustrative Cases

### The Dishwasher

Suppose the aim is to make the dishwasher available for dirty dishes after a wash cycle.

Fully unloading it produces a recognizable state: it can be loaded again. Half unloading it may leave someone unsure whether the remaining dishes are clean. Dirty dishes can then be added to clean ones.

The relevant difference is what the next person encounters. Completing the unloading avoids leaving that particular ambiguity.

This example illustrates the method. It does not establish a rule that every household task must always be completed immediately.

### An Unsuccessful Examination Attempt

A person fails an examination, reviews the errors, and prepares differently for the next attempt.

The first attempt is closed. The requirement remains open, with a next step. A specific lesson can be carried forward without continuing to treat the first attempt as unfinished work.

If the adjustment helps on the second attempt, the person has feedback about what was learned. If it does not, the interpretation can be reconsidered.

Choosing to leave the degree program would create a different ending. The method makes room for either path without deciding which life goal the person should pursue.

### The First Version of This Model

Completing a readable first version creates a state that did not exist during the conversation from which it developed: an identifiable text that can be used, examined, and criticized.

For this unit, the intended state is a sufficiently developed account with a recognizable purpose, working method, assumptions, and limits. Practical testing and further development remain open.

A later version may correct or reject parts of this one. The first version may have contributed by making those parts visible enough to examine.

The writing task for one version can therefore close while the model's development continues. This example documents the method's role in its own development.

## Assumptions, Uncertainty, and Limits

The method rests on working assumptions:

- People can influence at least some of the states they leave behind.
- Attention to resulting states can reveal relevant differences that are otherwise overlooked.
- In shared processes, recognizable states can reduce certain misunderstandings and coordination demands.
- Experience and feedback can improve the assessment of future states.

The examples make these assumptions understandable. They do not amount to systematic evidence of effectiveness across domains.

Some uncertainty can be reduced by checking information, such as remaining requirements or responsibilities. Other uncertainty requires practical use, including whether this method helps a particular person. Unexpected future events may remain uncertain despite careful handling.

This note does not supply a general theory of decision-making, emotional processing, or a good life. It does not define which goals people should have or promise control over outcomes.

Someone can act appropriately and still fail. A later difficulty does not by itself prove that an earlier ending was handled badly.

The handling of endings can differ by domain. Someone may handle professional endings well and everyday organization poorly. Cooperation, assistance, and delegation can contribute to usable states.

## Use, Observe, and Revise

The relevant practical question is whether the method improves the handling of a situation enough to justify its effort, compared with the person's usual approach.

Useful observations might include fewer misunderstandings, easier resumption of work, a clearer remaining obligation, or a lesson that becomes available in a later attempt. Additional effort without a useful difference also matters.

Before changing the model after an unexpected result, examine whether the state was misunderstood, an expectation was wrong, the application was unsuitable, or external circumstances changed. Revise the part implicated by the observation.

My broader expectation is that many well-handled endings can provide a more solid basis for life over time. This remains a guiding proposition rather than a demonstrated conclusion of this version.

The image behind this expectation is that past experience can become steps that support us. The states we leave behind help shape the ground from which we continue.
